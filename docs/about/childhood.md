<h1 style="color: #DC4D01;">Early Life</h1>


Growing up in Naples, Italy, I was surrounded by the city’s rich artistic heritage and vibrant culture, which sparked my early love for visual art. From a young age, I found myself drawn to drawing, sketching, and experimenting with color and form. This passion only grew as I discovered digital tools, which opened up a whole new world of creative possibilities.

My early years were filled with a mix of traditional art practice and self-taught exploration in graphic design. I would spend hours studying the details of my favorite illustrations and experimenting with different software, slowly building my skills and developing a personal style. The support of my family and teachers encouraged me to pursue art seriously, leading me to enroll at the Accademy of Fine arts of Naples.

These formative years gave me a strong foundation in both the classic and modern aspects of art, fueling my drive to explore the possibilities within the fields of design and illustration.


<h1 style="color: #DC4D01;">My Journey at the Academy: Blending Media Theory and Pop Culture</h1>
Looking back at my first year in the New Technologies of Art (NTA) program at the Academy of Fine Arts of Naples, it feels like a lifetime ago. Over the past two years, my artistic identity has evolved significantly. Moving from foundational sketches to complex digital systems, my time here has been defined by a deep dive into transmedia storytelling, interactive design, and the intersection of nostalgia and modern media.

One of the absolute highlights of this journey took place during my second year, during the *Teoria e Metodo dei Mass Media* (Mass Media Theory and Methods) course. The exam was held by Professor Vinceslao Cembalo—widely known as the co-director of the iconic Italian children's TV show *Melevisione*. Presenting a project to someone with such a massive background in media and television format creation was both thrilling and challenging.

For this exam, I decided to push the boundaries of traditional media analysis by creating an interactive, conceptual project heavily inspired by the pop culture universes that shaped my generation: *Yu-Gi-Oh!* and *Pokémon*. 

The core of the project revolved around a custom-designed trading card game system. I designed a set of fully custom cards, blending tactical gameplay with deep narrative symbolism. The plot and mechanics borrowed the high-stakes tension of Yu-Gi-Oh’s "Shadow Realm" (Regno delle Ombre) and fused it with the collection, evolution, and battling mechanics of Pokémon. It wasn't just a game; it was a commentary on how mass media creates immersive subcultures and psychological spaces where players invest real emotional weight.

Professor Cembalo was incredibly impressed and genuinely surprised by the depth of the work. He praised how a pop-culture phenomenon could be dissected and re-engineered into a sophisticated piece of media art, highlighting the balance between technical execution and narrative theory.

Receiving that kind of validation from a seasoned media director was a turning point. It proved to me that the boundary between "high art" and geek culture is completely blurred, and that contemporary nostalgia is one of the most powerful tools a digital artist can weaponize. As I approach the end of my third year, I feel more ready than ever to keep exploring where game design, illustration, and media theory collide.



<h1 style="color: #DC4D01;">Interactive Systems: From Hardware to Parametric Geometry</h1>

The conclusion of the academic semester marks the official end of the Interactive Systems course, one of the most challenging and rewarding journeys of this academic year. Reflecting on this experience, it has truly been an exploration of breaking down the wall between the physical environment we inhabit and the digital space where we design.

The final project emerged from a specific challenge: allowing physical matter and sensor data to directly communicate with pure generative mathematics. Using an Arduino Uno R4 Minima board, I explored how real-time environmental data streams could become the driving force behind the metamorphosis of a digital geometric structure.

By utilizing the UDP network protocol and the Firefly plugin, I bridged the hardware pipeline directly into Grasshopper. I programmed an algorithm that generates a responsive grid of parametric hexagons that adapt fluidly in real time—calculating updates every 10 milliseconds—based on the sensitivity and inputs of the sensor. The result is an interactive installation where form is no longer static, but morphs, scales, and shifts much like a living organism.

For those interested in exploring the mathematical logic behind the matrix, the behavior of the attractor point, or how the Python script and the UDP pipeline handle the incoming data stream, I have compiled a comprehensive technical analysis.
